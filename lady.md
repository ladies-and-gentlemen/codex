# The Lady's Code

## I. Character and Dignity

1. A lady is distinguished above all by her character. Manners, dress, beauty, and position may adorn a woman, but they cannot take the place of kindness, honesty, and dignity.

2. She respects herself and therefore does not seek respect through vanity, arrogance, or a constant desire to be noticed.

3. She does not try to appear more educated, wealthy, influential, or distinguished than she is.

4. Her dignity is shown most clearly where no one expects courtesy from her and where good conduct promises her no advantage.

5. She remembers that true nobility is determined not by birth, money, or fame, but by character and by the way one treats others.

## II. Modesty and Self-command

6. A lady's modesty lies not in timidity, but in the absence of conceit, importunity, and the desire to put herself on display.

7. She governs her temper and does not regard a bad mood as sufficient reason to treat others badly.

8. She does not raise her voice without need, make scenes, or make those around her pay for her irritation.

9. In a difficult situation, she endeavours to preserve calmness and presence of mind.

10. Restraint does not mean lack of feeling. A lady may feel deeply without turning every feeling into a public spectacle.

11. She does not rush to answer in anger and knows that a few minutes of silence may sometimes preserve what a long argument could destroy.

## III. Speech and Conversation

12. A lady knows how to speak, but she knows equally well how to listen.

13. She does not interrupt another person or continually look at her telephone while someone is speaking directly to her.

14. She does not monopolise a conversation or turn a company into an audience for herself.

15. She avoids subjects and questions that may needlessly embarrass, humiliate, or hurt someone present.

16. She does not seek a reputation for wit at the expense of another person's feelings. A joke after which someone must conceal hurt does no credit to the person who made it.

17. She does not correct every small mistake made by another person or display her knowledge merely to expose another's ignorance.

18. She speaks of those who are absent with the same measure of fairness that she would use in their presence.

19. Rudeness does not become wit merely because it is delivered with a smile.

## IV. Conduct in Society

20. A lady seeks to make company more agreeable not by demanding attention for herself, but by noticing others.

21. She does not leave a newcomer, a shy person, or someone little acquainted with the company in awkward isolation when she can naturally draw that person into the conversation.

22. She treats an influential person and one from whom she has nothing to gain with equal courtesy.

23. She does not emphasise differences of wealth, position, education, profession, or origin.

24. She knows how to receive a kindness with gratitude and does not regard the attention of others as something to which she is entitled.

25. She preserves good manners not only with strangers, but at home among family and those closest to her.

26. She knows that true breeding is seen especially clearly when there is no need to make an impression.

## V. Tact, Confidence, and Good Name

27. A lady possesses tact: she knows how to notice what ought not to be noticed aloud.

28. A confidence entrusted to her does not become less sacred after a quarrel, a cooling of friendship, or the end of an acquaintance.

29. She does not collect other people's confidences for the pleasure of passing them on.

30. She does not encourage malicious gossip or lend credibility to a rumour merely by repeating it.

31. She values a good name, both her own and another's, and does not damage anyone's reputation through a careless insinuation, publication, or unverified story.

32. She understands the difference between sincerity and tactlessness: not every truth need be spoken, and not every truth need be spoken now.

## VI. Appearance, Dress, and Taste

33. A lady's dress is suited to the time, place, and occasion.

34. Elegance lies for her not in the expense of her clothes or in the desire to attract every eye, but in cleanliness, good grooming, harmony, and good taste.

35. She does not seek to astonish with luxury where such display would make others uncomfortable.

36. She takes care of her attractiveness, but does not turn attractiveness into an instrument of vanity, rivalry, or deliberate control over others.

37. She does not make another person's appearance, age, figure, clothing, or lack of means a subject of ridicule.

38. She remembers that fashion should serve a woman, not a woman fashion.

## VII. Hospitality

39. A lady receiving guests remembers that the chief ornament of a home is not the richness of its furnishings, but the calmness and cordiality of its hostess.

40. In inviting someone into her home, she accepts the duty of doing everything reasonably possible to ensure that the guest does not feel superfluous or forgotten.

41. She does not display domestic troubles, irritation, or anxiety over imperfect preparations before her guests.

42. She introduces people who do not know one another and helps conversation begin naturally.

43. As a guest, she respects another person's home, time, customs, and the rules established by the hosts.

44. She gives thanks for hospitality, a gift, or a service sincerely and in good time.

## VIII. Home and Everyday Order

45. A lady regards the home not merely as a place in which to live, but as a place whose atmosphere is influenced by her own character.

46. She values order, cleanliness, foresight, and sensible economy without turning household concerns into a constant source of anxiety for everyone else.

47. She does not allow small domestic inconveniences to destroy peace within the family.

48. She remembers that comfort is created not only by things, but by tone of voice, patience, habits, and the way people treat one another.

49. A good home gives its inhabitants above all a sense of order, safety, warmth, and peace.

## IX. Conduct Towards Those Who Provide a Service

50. Older books of manners often judged a lady's breeding by the way she treated servants. In the modern world, the same principle applies to everyone whose work makes her life more convenient.

51. She speaks with equal respect to domestic helpers, nannies, waiters, shop assistants, drivers, couriers, cleaners, hotel staff, and others who provide her with a service.

52. She does not humiliate a person merely because that person's work requires carrying out her request or order.

53. If a mistake has been made, she addresses it calmly and plainly, without turning a legitimate complaint into an opportunity to humiliate another person.

54. She gives thanks for work well done and remembers that another person's labour does not become invisible merely because it has been paid for.

55. A person's breeding is especially easy to judge by the way that person treats those who cannot readily answer in the same tone.

## X. Friendship and Charity

56. A lady is loyal to her friends not only when friendship is pleasant or advantageous.

57. She does not envy the good fortune of a woman close to her, nor seek consolation in that woman's misfortune.

58. She does not take part in bullying or join in mockery of an absent person merely to win the approval of a company.

59. She helps a person in need in such a way that, as far as possible, the help does not humiliate the person receiving it.

60. She does not turn charity into a display of her own virtue.

61. She would rather reconcile people than set them against one another, and she does not repeat a harsh remark merely to see what quarrel it may cause.

## XI. Courtship, Love, and Marriage

62. In her relations with a man, a lady combines naturalness with dignity and does not seek attention through provocative conduct.

63. She does not encourage serious hope where she knows that she has no intention of returning it.

64. She does not use her attractiveness, jealousy, silence, or flirtation to manipulate another person deliberately.

65. In choosing a companion for life, she looks not only at appearance, means, and position, but above all at character, constancy, kindness, disposition, and the ability to fulfil one's duties.

66. She does not make the private details of courtship, love, or family relationships a source of entertainment for outsiders.

67. In marriage, she does not turn small disagreements into a struggle for superiority.

68. She does not sacrifice her husband's dignity, or the private matters of family life entrusted to her, to a moment's irritation, gossip, or a desire to win the sympathy of others.

## XII. One's Word, Letters, and Messages

69. Once, a woman's letters revealed much about her upbringing. Today, the same is true of letters, messages, electronic mail, and correspondence on the internet.

70. The written word should be as courteous as personal speech: clear, sincere, and free from needless harshness.

71. A lady answers invitations, important letters, and arrangements in good time and does not leave another person indefinitely waiting for her decision.

72. In strong anger, she does not send a message that she would be ashamed to say calmly at a personal meeting.

73. She attaches importance to her word and does not regard a promise as optional merely because it has not been written down or legally formalised.

## XIII. The Telephone, the Internet, and Social Media

74. A telephone serves a lady; it does not command her attention.

75. At table, during a conversation, a visit, or a meeting, she does not allow a screen continually to become more important than the people present with her.

76. She does not photograph or publish another person in an embarrassing situation for amusement or attention.

77. Private correspondence remains private. She does not forward another person's messages or photographs without sufficient reason and permission.

78. She does not turn a family quarrel, a disagreement between friends, or disappointment in love into a public performance.

79. She does not write on the internet what she would not dare to say to a person directly while looking that person in the eye.

80. She does not take the number of followers, views, or approving reactions as a measure of her own worth.

81. She avoids the habit of displaying every virtue, gift, good deed, or happy moment before an audience merely to confirm her position.

82. Good taste extends also to what a person chooses to leave unseen.

## XIV. Education and Accomplishments

83. A lady develops her mind, taste, and abilities throughout her life.

84. Reading, music, languages, history, art, crafts, and other accomplishments are valuable not as means of showing off, but as ways of enriching her own life and the company of those around her.

85. She does not parade her education or pretend to know what she does not know.

86. True education makes her conversation more substantial, her taste more refined, and her judgement more careful, rather than increasing her conceit.

87. She retains curiosity and the ability to learn regardless of age.

## XV. Study, Work, and Duties

88. At school, university, and work, a lady approaches her duties conscientiously.

89. She does not pass off another person's work as her own or regard dishonesty as acceptable merely because it is difficult to detect.

90. She respects the time of teachers, colleagues, friends, and other people just as she would wish her own time to be respected.

91. She arrives punctually or gives notice when circumstances prevent her from keeping an arrangement.

92. She endeavours to do well even the work for which no praise will follow.

## XVI. A Lady Away from Home

93. In the street, while travelling, at school, university, work, the theatre, a restaurant, a hotel, as a guest, or in any public place, a lady remembers that her own convenience should not needlessly become an inconvenience to others.

94. She does not seek attention through loudness, provocative behaviour, or deliberate eccentricity.

95. She treats public places and the property of others with care.

96. Even far from those who know her, she preserves the same standards of conduct, because good breeding does not exist for the sake of witnesses.

## XVII. The Principal Rule

97. To be a lady does not mean never to make a mistake. It means being able to notice one's mistake, admit it, and endeavour to put it right.

98. Good manners are not a collection of ceremonies. They become the natural outward expression of good character, self-respect, and consideration for others.

99. A lady does not continually try to prove to others that she is a lady. The more genuine the breeding, the less it needs to be displayed.

100. And the whole code may be reduced to one principle:

**A lady is not the woman whose manners are impeccable only when she is being watched.
A lady is the woman whose inner dignity naturally becomes good manners.
Her presence does not demand attention, but brings calm; her gentleness is not without character, her attractiveness needs no provocative display, and her nobility is seen most clearly in the way she treats those before whom she has nothing to prove.**
