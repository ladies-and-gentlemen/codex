# The Gentleman's Code

## I. Honour and One's Word

1. A gentleman keeps his word. If he makes a promise, he fulfils it. If fulfilment becomes impossible, he says so himself rather than waiting to be reminded.

2. He does not promise what he has neither the intention nor the ability to deliver.

3. His honesty does not depend on the likelihood of being caught.

4. He does not use technical correctness, a loophole, or another person's trust to justify conduct that is plainly dishonourable.

5. He recognises an obligation even when there is no written contract and no one can compel him to fulfil it.

6. A gentleman's word has value precisely because his honour stands behind it.

## II. Responsibility and Duty

7. A gentleman takes responsibility for his decisions and their consequences.

8. When he makes a mistake, he admits it without searching for a convenient person to blame and, as far as possible, puts right the harm he has caused.

9. He does not shift responsibility onto someone who is weaker, dependent upon him, or unable to answer back.

10. He distinguishes what he wants from what duty requires and is capable of doing what is necessary even when it is inconvenient.

11. Having undertaken a task, he endeavours to see it through; if that becomes impossible, he honestly warns those who are relying upon him.

12. He expects of himself before he expects of others.

## III. Self-command

13. A gentleman governs himself. Anger may explain an action, but it does not excuse it.

14. He does not turn every slight into a conflict, nor every disagreement into hostility.

15. Tiredness, irritation, and a bad mood do not give him licence to treat others badly.

16. He can lose without belittling the victor and win without humiliating the defeated.

17. He does not answer petty meanness with greater meanness, nor does he regard revenge as proof of strength.

18. He does not allow gambling, amusement, a bad habit, or any other passion to become master of his will.

## IV. Dignity and Conduct Towards Others

19. A gentleman preserves his own dignity without diminishing another person's.

20. He is courteous not only to those who may be useful to him.

21. Birth, wealth, position, fame, and social connections are not, to him, measures of human dignity.

22. Strength, status, money, knowledge, and influence mean greater responsibility to him, not a greater licence for rudeness.

23. He does not demand respect through fear.

24. He does not exploit another person's weakness, inexperience, trust, confusion, or dependent position.

25. If he gains an advantage dishonestly, he does not regard it as a deserved victory.

26. He helps when help is genuinely needed and within his power, without turning a good deed into a performance of his own virtue.

## V. Women, Courtship, and Family

27. A gentleman treats a woman with respect, consideration, and natural courtesy, and does not permit himself degrading familiarity or rudeness.

28. Gallantry is neither a performance nor a means of displaying his own superiority. He offers help naturally and does not demand gratitude for it.

29. He does not force his company or courtship upon a woman when she has made it clear that they are unwelcome.

30. He does not play with another person's feelings or encourage serious hopes where he has no serious intentions himself.

31. He does not boast of his relationships with women or turn the private side of courtship or love into entertainment for his friends.

32. Letters, confidences, and personal circumstances entrusted to him by a woman remain confidential after a quarrel, separation, or the end of an acquaintance.

33. He values a woman's good name and does not damage it through a careless story, insinuation, or boast.

34. In choosing a wife, he looks not only to beauty, means, and position, but above all to character, kindness, sound judgement, and the ability to share the duties of family life.

35. In marriage he is faithful to his word, does not turn disagreements into contests for superiority, and does not display family quarrels before outsiders.

36. The courtesy he shows to women in society he preserves first of all towards his mother, wife, sisters, daughters, and other women close to him.

## VI. Friendship and Loyalty

37. A gentleman does not betray someone who has placed trust in him merely for convenience or advantage.

38. Loyalty to a friend does not require complicity in that friend's dishonourable conduct.

39. He can tell someone close to him an unwelcome truth, and he can defend that person against an unjust accusation.

40. He speaks of an absent friend in such a way that he would not be ashamed of his words were that friend to enter the room.

41. He does not abandon friends merely because their circumstances have worsened.

42. He does not choose friends solely for their usefulness, wealth, or ability to open the right doors.

## VII. Courage, Strength, and Readiness to Help

43. A gentleman's courage lies not in the absence of fear, but in the ability to do what is right despite fear.

44. He is capable of standing against the majority when the majority is acting dishonourably.

45. He never uses physical strength to humiliate someone weaker.

46. If a woman, child, elderly or ill person, or anyone else nearby genuinely needs protection or help, a gentleman does not pass by merely because intervening would be inconvenient.

47. He does not seek a fight in order to prove his courage, but neither does he disguise cowardice as prudence.

48. In a dangerous situation he endeavours to keep his head and be useful rather than add to the general fear.

49. He considers it fitting for a man to know at least the basics of first aid, to know how to summon help, and not to be wholly helpless in an ordinary emergency.

50. When helping another person, he does not demand a reward for an act he regarded as his duty.

## VIII. Speech and Conversation

51. A gentleman knows how not only to speak, but also to listen.

52. He does not speak more loudly merely because he has nothing further of substance to add.

53. He knows how to challenge an idea without trying to destroy the person who holds it.

54. He does not boast about what ought to speak for itself.

55. He does not tell humiliating stories about others merely to win approval in company.

56. He knows how to keep silent about what has been entrusted to him personally.

57. He does not mistake coarse or indecent speech for proof of maturity, courage, or manliness.

## IX. Conflict

58. A gentleman says to a person's face only what he is prepared to stand by in that person's absence.

59. He knows the difference between defending his honour and defending wounded vanity.

60. When a conflict can be ended with dignity, he does not prolong it merely to have the last word.

61. An apology is not a humiliation when he is in the wrong.

62. Having accepted a sincere apology, he does not preserve the old offence as a weapon for every future disagreement.

63. He does not gather a crowd against an opponent or resort to public humiliation where a matter can be settled between those directly involved.

## X. Money, Work, and Dealings

64. A gentleman repays his debts.

65. He does not make another person beg for what is rightfully theirs: payment, recognition for their work, the return of an item, or the fulfilment of a promise.

66. In business and other dealings, he does not exploit the other party's ignorance to obtain an advantage he knows to be unfair.

67. An agreement matters to him even when it cannot be proved in court.

68. He pays fairly and on time for the work of others.

69. He respects another person's time: he arrives punctually or gives notice that he will be late.

70. He does not pretend to wealth he does not possess, and he understands the difference between generosity and extravagance.

71. He does not recklessly risk resources for which he is responsible not only to himself, but also to his family or to others.

## XI. Appearance and Taste

72. A gentleman attends to cleanliness, neatness, and his appearance.

73. His dress suits the place, the time, and the occasion.

74. Elegance, to him, lies not in the price of things, the display of luxury, or the desire to attract every eye, but in good taste, neatness, and propriety.

75. He does not mock another person's clothing, appearance, or lack of means to buy expensive things.

76. He regards care for himself as part of respect for himself and for others, not as an occasion for vanity.

## XII. Manners, Society, and Hospitality

77. Good manners exist not to display one's upbringing, but to ease social intercourse and avoid causing others unnecessary inconvenience.

78. A gentleman knows how to be both a good host and a good guest.

79. When receiving people, he thinks first of their comfort rather than of displaying his means.

80. In company he notices the newcomer, the shy person, or someone left on the edge of the group and, when appropriate, helps them enter the conversation.

81. In a public place he does not regard his own convenience as more important than everyone else's peace: he does not jostle, make needless noise, or disregard queues and established rules.

82. He offers a seat or assistance to someone who plainly needs it more: a woman with a child, an elderly or ill person, someone carrying a heavy load, or anyone in evident difficulty.

83. Small courtesies — holding a door, helping with something heavy, allowing another person to go first when appropriate — are offered naturally and without theatrics.

84. He gives thanks for an invitation, hospitality, a gift, and a service rendered, and does not regard another person's care as something to which he is simply entitled.

## XIII. Those Who Serve and Provide Services

85. Older guides judged a gentleman particularly by the way he treated servants. The same principle applies to everyone whose work makes his life easier.

86. He speaks with equal respect to domestic staff, waiters, shop assistants, drivers, couriers, cleaners, hotel staff, and other people providing him with a service.

87. He does not humiliate a person merely because their position or work requires them to carry out an instruction, request, or order.

88. If a mistake has been made, he addresses it calmly and clearly, without turning a justified complaint into an opportunity to vent his irritation.

89. When directing the work of others, he remembers that authority over a person's work is not authority over that person's dignity.

## XIV. Education, Practical Ability, and Health

90. A gentleman develops his mind and continues to learn regardless of age.

91. He does not pretend to know what he does not know, and can calmly say, ‘I do not know.’

92. At school, university, and work he does not present another person's work as his own, nor does he use new technologies as a means of concealing his own dishonesty.

93. He strives to be not only educated but practically capable: able to look after himself, maintain order, deal with an ordinary household task, and help another person.

94. He takes care of his health, cleanliness, and physical fitness not for self-admiration, but so that he may command his own body and not become helpless through neglect.

95. In sport, games, and competition he observes the rules even when a breach would be difficult to detect. Victory by cheating is not a gentleman's victory.

## XV. Telephone, Internet, and Private Life

96. A telephone and other devices serve a gentleman; they do not command his attention. During a face-to-face conversation, a shared meal, a lesson, or a meeting, he does not allow a screen without good reason to become more important than the people present.

97. In a letter, a message, and online, he follows the same rules of honour and courtesy that would govern him in conversation face to face.

98. He does not publish or forward another person's private correspondence, photographs, confidences, or embarrassing moments for amusement, boasting, or revenge.

99. He does not write in anger what he would be ashamed to say calmly to a person face to face, does not join public persecution merely for the approval of the crowd, and does not measure his own dignity by the amount of attention he receives online.

## XVI. The Principal Rule

100. A gentleman endeavours to leave a person, a conversation, a matter, and a place **no worse than they were before his arrival and, where possible, a little better**.

And finally:

**A gentleman is not the man who knows which fork to use.
A gentleman is the man whose honour does not depend upon witnesses, whose strength does not require the humiliation of the weak, and whose good manners are the natural continuation of good character.
In his presence, the weak need not be afraid, an equal need not feel diminished, and the strong need not prove their strength.**
